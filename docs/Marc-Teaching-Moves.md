# How Marc Teaches: Moves to Build Interactive Lessons From

The worksheets show the prompts. The teaching is what happens live: the questions asked, the order things appear, and how mistakes are handled. An interactive lesson has to replace the teacher, so every section needs these moves written down in Marc's own method.

---

## Principles (Marc's own)

- **Build up to the full problem.** Lessons never start with the full problem. Students work up to it.
- **The vocabulary is used, not just heard.** Students learn the language behind the algebra (coefficient, constant, rate, variable) so they can talk about structure.
- **The constant is how much there is when n = 0.**
- **Stage matters.** By the patterns-in-context stage (Pattern Bridge P3), students already solve equations with inverse operations and know coefficient and constant. Lessons at that stage apply the algebra; they don't introduce it.
- **Let them figure it out.** Once students have the tools, they find the error themselves and explain it. Explaining is assessed as communication (MYP).
- **In an interactive version, students touch the parts.** The constant is one colour and the coefficient groups another, and students build each rule with those pieces to see what it makes.

## Recorded moves

### 1. Finding the explicit rule of a linear sequence (row comparison)

Example pattern: 5, 8, 11, 14, 17.

1. Make a table. The first row is n: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5. The second row is the pattern.
2. Find the difference between terms: it goes up by 3. That is the coefficient.
3. Write the "normal" row below it: what the pattern would be if it were just skip counting by 3 (3, 6, 9, 12, 15). Students can fill it in.
4. Ask: what is the 10th term of the normal row? 30. How did you know? 10 × 3. And 10 is n, so the normal row is 3n.
5. But the pattern isn't 3n. Ask: how far off is each term? Compare row by row: every term is 2 more.
6. Ask: if n is 0, how much is it? 2. So the constant is how much there is when n = 0.
7. The rule is 3n + 2.

### 2. Showing the constant and the coefficient in a growing figure (colour)

Colour the constant one colour and each group of the coefficient a second colour. If the constant is 3 and the coefficient is 5, Figure 1 shows 3 tiles of the first colour and 5 of the second. Figures 2, 3 and 4 each show one extra group of 5 in the second colour, so students see what stays the same and what grows.

### 3. Testing a rule

Students may think that if a rule works for one figure, it's correct. The answer is "Maybe. We should always try another one," such as n = 0 or n = 10. For a single-variable rule, if it works for three figures, it is correct.

### 4. When a student overcounts shared parts (sticks in a row of squares)

A student says Figure 10 needs 40 sticks, because each square has 4. Test the claim on smaller figures, one step at a time:

1. "If that's true, Figure 2 has 8 sticks. Count them." 7. "Wait. Why?"
2. "Then how many does Figure 3 have? Is it just one less, so 11?" Count: 10. "So it didn't add 4 new ones? Why not?"
3. "So the 2nd square added 3, and the 3rd square added 3."
4. "Does the first one have the same pattern added on? If so, that's 1 + 3. The 2nd is 1 + two 3s. The 3rd is 1 + three 3s." That matches the count of 10.
5. "So how many would the 10th one have?"

### 5. Showing two rules are the same (equivalent expressions)

Example: S = 3n + 1 and S = 4 + 3(n − 1).

1. Have students substitute 2 or 3 values into each rule and see that they give the same results. Ask: are these two rules the same?
2. Remind them of the distributive property, learned in earlier lessons.
3. Have them simplify the second rule. If they do it correctly, they see it is the first rule.
4. Predict the mistakes they can make when simplifying, such as not distributing to both terms in the bracket or not combining like terms.

### 6. Subtraction by constant difference (from the Evidence Bank)

Add the same amount to both numbers until the number being subtracted is a round number: 503 − 268 becomes 505 − 270, then 535 − 300. Show the rule works with small cases first, such as 4 − 2 = 6 − 4.

### 7. Regrouping (from the Evidence Bank)

A story about borrowing milk. Being extended to a bottle for ones, a crate of 10 bottles for tens, and a truck of 10 crates for hundreds.

The interactive P3 needs Marc's method for each of these. Dictate them in any order.

1. **The fixed fee in context.** The workshop table starts at 1 participant and shows 52. How do you get students to see 52 isn't the fee? Do you use the row comparison again?
2. **Three kinds of change.** Bottles, the water tank, the seedlings. How do you get students to tell adding the same amount from multiplying by the same factor?
3. **Model limits.** The tank can't go below 0 litres. How do you raise the question of which inputs are allowed?
4. **The printers.** How do you lead students to where the two costs are equal, and to repair the claim "B is always cheaper"?
5. **Repairing 90 + 6(n − 1).** How do you explain what (n − 1) counts, and why the starting value has to change?
6. **Verify versus justify.** Answered: see move 3, testing a rule.
