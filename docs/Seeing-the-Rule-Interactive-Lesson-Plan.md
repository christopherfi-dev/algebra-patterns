# Seeing the Rule: Interactive Lesson Plan

**Working title.** An interactive lesson built from Marc's Visual Patterns lesson (tiles and sticks), with the evaluate-and-repair task from Pattern Bridge P2, the garden exit ticket and model limits from Pattern Bridge P3, Marc's row-comparison method, and an added climb to rate of change.

**Who it's for:** learners who already solve linear equations with inverse operations and know the words coefficient and constant (Grade 7 MYP after Lessons 1 to 6, or an adult reviewing algebra).

**What the learner leaves with:**
- a linear rule read straight from a picture: the coefficient is what each figure adds, and the constant is what is there at figure 0;
- the difference between checking a rule and justifying it for every figure;
- why a growing square can't have a straight-line rule;
- the speed of a ball at a single instant, found from a shrinking square border.

**Length:** 16 screens in seven parts.

**Marc's moves:** every screen that depends on how Marc teaches now uses his method, as recorded in Marc-Teaching-Moves. Screens 2, 6, 7, 8 and 10 are built directly on his answers.

---

## How every screen works

**Text:** at most two short sentences before the learner acts.

**Choosing the action:**

| Action | Used when | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Type | The number or rule is the evidence of thinking | Can't be guessed |
| Drag, place or tap | The arrangement is the idea (a group of tiles, a stick, a label) | The placement is the learning |
| Slide | Exploring what happens as n changes | Shows consequences |

**Building up:** a screen never shows the full problem at once. Rows and parts appear one step at a time.

**The Check button:**

| Outcome | What happens |
|---|---|
| Correct | A short confirmation naming what was right. **Why?** appears. |
| Predicted error | A response written for that specific mistake. **Why?** appears. |
| Unpredicted error | A hint toward the first step, without the answer. |
| Second miss | The screen demonstrates the step. The learner still enters the answer to continue. |

**Why?** appears only after the first Check, right or wrong, so it can't be used to skip the thinking. During the exit ticket it stays hidden until the learner submits.

**Accepted forms:** equivalent rules count (3n + 2 and 2 + 3n). Units are optional.

## Part 1: Warm-up

### Screen 1. Retrieval: is 103 in the pattern?

**Purpose:** confirm the algebra the lesson assumes.

**Shows:** "7, 11, 15, 19, …"

**Step A. Learner types** the explicit rule.
- **Correct (4n + 3):** "Right. Each term adds 4, and the value at n = 0 is 3."
- **7 + 4n:** "Test it at n = 1: it gives 11, but the first term is 7. 7 is the term at n = 1. The constant is the value at n = 0."
- **4n:** "4n gives 4, 8, 12, 16. Every term of the pattern is 3 more."
- **Unpredicted:** "What does each term add? Then find the value at n = 0."
- **Second miss (Marc's row comparison):** the table builds row by row.

| n | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pattern | 3 | 7 | 11 | 15 | 19 |
| "Normal" row (4n) | 0 | 4 | 8 | 12 | 16 |
| How far off | 3 | 3 | 3 | 3 | 3 |

**Step B. Learner types** which term is 103.
- **Correct (25):** "4n + 3 = 103 gives 4n = 100, so n = 25. 103 is the 25th term."
- **26 or 100:** "Solve 4n + 3 = 103. Undo the + 3 first, then the × 4."

**Why?** "Each term adds 4, so the coefficient is 4. The pattern is always 3 more than the 4 times table, and at n = 0 it would be 3, so the constant is 3. The rule is T = 4n + 3. To find which term is 103, solve 4n + 3 = 103: n = 25."

## Part 2: Tiles. See the structure

### Screen 2. What stays and what grows?

**Purpose:** find the fixed part and the growing part in a picture.

**Shows (Marc's colouring):** Figures 1, 2 and 3. The constant is one colour and every group of the coefficient is a second colour. Figure 1: 2 fixed tiles plus one group of 3. Figure 2: the same 2 fixed tiles plus two groups of 3. Figure 3: the same 2 plus three groups of 3. Each new figure shows one extra group in the second colour.

**Step A. Learner types** how many tiles of the first colour are in every figure.
- **Correct (2):** "The same 2 tiles, in every figure."
- **5:** "5 is all of Figure 1. Count only the colour that stays the same size."

**Step B. Learner types** how many tiles each new figure adds.
- **Correct (3):** "Each figure adds one group of 3 in the second colour."
- **5:** "5 is the whole of Figure 1. Compare Figure 1 and Figure 2: what was added?"

**Step C. Learner drags** one group of 3 to build Figure 4, then types its total.
- **Correct (14):** "4 groups of 3, plus the 2 fixed tiles: 14."
- **12:** "You counted the groups. The 2 fixed tiles are in every figure too."
- **20:** "Figure 1 has 5 tiles, but only 3 of them repeat."

**Why?** "Every figure has the same 2 fixed tiles. Each new figure adds one group of 3. So Figure 4 has 4 groups of 3 and the 2 fixed tiles: 12 + 2 = 14."

### Screen 3. Figure 0, and the rule

**Purpose:** the constant is what is there at figure 0.

**Step A. Learner slides** the figure number back to 0, then types how many tiles Figure 0 has.
- **Correct (2):** "Figure 0 has no groups, only the 2 fixed tiles."
- **5:** "5 is Figure 1. Take away its group of 3."
- **0:** "The groups are gone, but look at the picture: the fixed tiles stay."

**Step B. Learner types** the rule T = …
- **Correct (3n + 2):** "Right. Slide to check any figure."
- **5 + 3n:** the screen tests it at n = 1: 8, not 5. "5 is the total for Figure 1. The constant is the value at Figure 0."
- **5n:** tests n = 2: 10, not 8. "Only 3 tiles repeat in each figure, not 5."
- **Unpredicted:** "How many tiles does each figure add, and how many are there at Figure 0?"

**Why?** "The coefficient 3 counts the tiles in each group, so it multiplies the figure number n. The constant 2 counts the fixed tiles, which are there even at Figure 0. You can also see it in a table, the way you'd compare rows:"

| n | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tiles | 2 | 5 | 8 | 11 | 14 |
| "Normal" row (3n) | 0 | 3 | 6 | 9 | 12 |
| How far off | 2 | 2 | 2 | 2 | 2 |

"The picture and the table give the same constant. T = 3n + 2."

### Screen 4. Why does Figure 1 have 5 tiles if the constant is 2?

**Purpose:** the constant is not the first total.

**Learner chooses** the explanation:
- **Correct:** "Figure 1 has one group of 3 plus the 2 fixed tiles. The constant counts only the fixed tiles."
- **"The rule must be wrong for Figure 1":** "Check it: 3 × 1 + 2 = 5. The rule is right. The first total and the constant are different things."
- **"The constant is the first total minus 3 by coincidence":** "It isn't coincidence. The first total always includes one group; the constant is what's there before any group."

**Why?** "The first total includes one group, so it is the constant plus the coefficient: 2 + 3 = 5. The constant is the value at Figure 0, before any groups are added. Reading the first total as the constant is the most common mistake with these rules."

### Screen 5. Which figure has 23 tiles?

**Purpose:** a reverse question: from a total back to the figure number.

**Learner types** which figure has 23 tiles.
- **Correct (7):** "3n + 2 = 23 gives 3n = 21, so n = 7."
- **Unpredicted:** "Undo the + 2 first, then the × 3."

**Why?** "The rule turns a figure number into a total. To go backwards, set the rule equal to the total and solve: 3n + 2 = 23, so 3n = 21 and n = 7. Figure 7 has 23 tiles: 7 groups of 3, plus the 2 fixed tiles."

### Screen 6. Does one check prove it?

**Purpose:** one matching figure isn't enough. Test more figures, including n = 0 and a figure far away. (Marc's move)

**Shows:** "A student says the rule is T = 5n, because Figure 1 has 5 tiles."

**Step A. Learner types** what T = 5n gives for Figure 1, then chooses: "Is the rule correct?"
- **Types 5, chooses "Maybe":** "Maybe. Always try another one."
- **Types 5, chooses "Yes":** "It works for Figure 1, but one figure isn't enough. Try another one."

**Step B. Learner types** what T = 5n gives for Figure 0 and Figure 10. The picture shows the real counts beside them.
- **Correct (0 and 50):** "Figure 0 has 2 tiles, not 0. Figure 10 has 32, not 50. The rule is wrong."

**Step C. Learner types** what T = 3n + 2 gives for Figures 0, 1 and 10.
- **Correct (2, 5 and 32):** "It works for all three, including Figure 0 and a figure far away."
- **Any slip:** "Substitute again: 3 × n, then add 2."

**Step D. Learner chooses** why we can trust 3n + 2 for every figure:
- **Correct:** "It works for three figures, and every figure adds exactly one more group of 3 to the same 2 fixed tiles."
- **"It worked for Figure 1":** "So did 5n. One figure can fit a wrong rule."

**Why?** "One figure can fit a wrong rule: 5n and 3n + 2 both give 5 at Figure 1. Another check exposes the wrong one. Figure 0 is a good test because it checks the constant, the tiles there before any group. A figure far away, like Figure 10, checks the coefficient, because a wrong coefficient drifts further off with every figure. When a pattern adds the same amount every time and a straight-line rule works for three figures, it works for all of them."

## Part 3: Sticks. Two ways to see one rule

### Screen 7. Shared edges

**Purpose:** a new picture with a new trap.

**Shows:** squares made of equal sticks in a straight row, neighbouring squares sharing an edge. Figure 1: 1 square. Figure 2: 2 squares. Figure 3: 3 squares.

**Step A. Learner drags** sticks to build Figure 4. A stick dropped on an existing edge is rejected: "That edge is already there."
- **Then types the total (13):** "Each new square needs only 3 sticks. It shares one with its neighbour."

**Step B. Learner types** the total for Figure 10.
- **Correct (31):** "1 + 3 × 10 = 31."
- **30:** "3 for each square. Now look at Figure 1: does it follow the same pattern? It's 1 + 3."
- **40 (Marc's move: test the claim on smaller figures, one step at a time):**
  1. "If each square has 4 sticks, how many would Figure 2 have?" Learner types 8.
  2. "Count them." Figure 2's sticks light up one by one: 7. "Why?" The shared edge flashes.
  3. "Then how many does Figure 3 have?" Learner types a prediction.
     - **11 ("one less"):** "Count them." The sticks light up: 10. "So it didn't add 4 new ones. Why not?" The shared edges flash.
     - **10:** "Right."
  4. "How many sticks did the 2nd square add? The 3rd?" Learner types 3 and 3.
  5. "Does the first one follow the same pattern? If so, Figure 1 is 1 + 3." Then: "Figure 2 is 1 + two 3s. Figure 3 is…?" Learner types 10: "1 + three 3s. That matches the count."
  6. "So how many does Figure 10 have?" Learner types 31.

**Why?** "Every square after the first adds only 3 sticks, because it shares an edge with the square before it. The first square follows the same pattern if you see it as 1 stick plus 3. So Figure 1 is 1 + 3, Figure 2 is 1 + two 3s, Figure 3 is 1 + three 3s, and Figure 10 is 1 + ten 3s = 31."

### Screen 8. Are these two rules the same?

**Purpose:** equivalent rules give the same value for every n. Show it by substitution first, then by simplifying. (Marc's move)

**Shows:** "Two rules for the sticks: S = 3n + 1 and S = 4 + 3(n − 1). Are these two rules the same?"

**Step A. Learner types** the value of each rule for n = 1, 2 and 3.
- **Correct (4, 7, 10 for both):** "The same values for all three. Are they the same for every n? Simplify the second rule to find out."
- **Slip in 4 + 3(n − 1):** "Work inside the bracket first, then multiply by 3, then add 4."

**Step B.** A reminder card appears: "The distributive property: 3(n − 1) = 3 × n − 3 × 1." **Learner types** 4 + 3(n − 1) simplified. Every wrong answer is tested against the original by substitution, so the learner sees where it breaks.
- **Correct (3n + 1):** "4 + 3n − 3 = 3n + 1. The two rules are the same."
- **3n + 3:** "The 3 multiplies both terms in the bracket. You multiplied the n but not the 1." Test at n = 1: 6, but the original gives 4.
- **3n + 7:** "3 × (−1) is −3, not +3." Test at n = 1: 10, but the original gives 4.
- **7n − 7:** "You added 4 + 3 before multiplying. Multiply 3(n − 1) first." Test: at n = 2 both give 7, but at n = 1 yours gives 0 and the original gives 4. "It matched one figure. One substitution isn't enough."
- **7n − 3:** "4 and 3n aren't like terms. Only the numbers 4 and −3 combine." Test: at n = 1 both give 4, but at n = 2 yours gives 11 and the original gives 7.
- **4 + 3n − 3 (not combined):** "Correct so far. Now combine the numbers 4 and −3."
- **Unpredicted:** "Distribute first: 3 × n and 3 × (−1). Then combine the numbers."

**Step C. Learner slides** to Figure 0 and types how many sticks are left.
- **Correct (1):** "One lone stick: the constant in 3n + 1."
- **4:** "4 is Figure 1. Take away its three sticks."

**Why?** "Substituting n = 1, 2 and 3 showed the two rules agree for three figures. Simplifying shows why they agree for every figure: 4 + 3(n − 1) = 4 + 3n − 3 = 3n + 1. In the picture, 4 + 3(n − 1) counts the first full square, then 3 sticks for each square after it. 3n + 1 counts one starting stick, then 3 for every square. Same sticks, grouped differently."

### Screen 9. Forward and backward

**Step A. Learner types** the sticks in Figure 40.
- **Correct (121):** "3 × 40 + 1 = 121."

**Step B. Learner types** which figure uses exactly 100 sticks.
- **Correct (33):** "3n + 1 = 100 gives 3n = 99, so n = 33."
- **Unpredicted:** "Undo the + 1 first, then the × 3."

**Why?** "Forward, the rule takes a figure number and gives the sticks: Figure 40 needs 3 × 40 + 1 = 121. Backward, set the rule equal to the sticks and solve: 3n + 1 = 100, so n = 33."

## Part 4: Evaluate and repair

### Screen 10. Three students, three rules

**Purpose:** by this point learners have the tools to find the error themselves. They build each rule with their hands and see what it makes, instead of being told. (Marc's approach)

**Shows:** "Three students wrote rules for the sticks." Mina: S = 3n + 1. Arun: S = 4 + 3n. Nina: S = 4 + 3(n − 1). Sticks in the two colours from earlier: the constant in one colour, each group of 3 in the second colour.

**Step A. Learner builds** Mina's rule for Figure 1 by touching and dragging: 1 stick in the constant colour, then 1 group of 3 in the second colour.
- **Result:** one square. "That's Figure 1."

**Step B. Learner builds** Arun's rule for Figure 1: 4 sticks in the constant colour, then 1 group of 3.
- **Learner types** how many squares they built: 2. Then which figure that is: Figure 2.
- **Response:** "Arun's rule built Figure 2 when it should have built Figure 1."

**Step C. Learner builds** Nina's rule for Figure 1: 4 sticks in the constant colour, then 3 × (1 − 1) = 0 groups.
- **Result:** one square. "That's Figure 1."

**Step D. Learner marks** each rule correct or incorrect.
- **Correct:** Mina correct, Arun incorrect, Nina correct.
- **Arun marked correct:** "Build his rule for Figure 1 again and count the squares."

**Step E. Explain it (not scored).** "In your own words, why is Arun's rule one figure ahead?" The learner writes, then sees a model explanation beside their own and ticks whether theirs says the same thing: "Arun's 4 is already a whole square. Then he adds 3 more sticks for every square, so he counts one square too many."

**Why?** "Arun's constant, 4, is already a complete square. His 3n then adds a three-stick piece for every square, so at n = 1 he builds two squares: Figure 2. Mina starts from 1 stick, so n pieces of 3 make exactly n squares. Nina keeps the full first square but uses (n − 1), so she only adds pieces for the squares after the first. Her rule simplifies to Mina's: equivalent rules can look different and still give the same value for every n."

## Part 5: When the growth grows

### Screen 11. Square figures

**Purpose:** a pattern with no common difference, seen in a picture.

**Shows:** square figures of tiles: 1 × 1, 2 × 2, 3 × 3.

**Step A. Learner types** the tiles in Figure 4.
- **Correct (16).**
- **12:** "That's adding 3 again. Build it: a 4-by-4 square."

**Step B. Learner drags** the L-shaped border that turns each square into the next. The borders appear: 3, 5, 7. Learner types the next border.
- **Correct (9):** "Each border is 2 tiles longer than the one before."
- **7:** "That's the border from 3 × 3 to 4 × 4. The next one turns 4 × 4 into 5 × 5."

**Step C. Learner chooses:** "Is there a common difference?"
- **Correct (No).**

**Why?** "Each new square adds an L-shaped border around two sides. The borders are 3, 5, 7, 9: they grow by 2 every time, because each side of the square is 1 longer. With no common difference, this pattern can't be arithmetic."

### Screen 12. Can a line fit?

**Shows:** the points 1, 4, 9, 16 and dials for a and b in T = an + b. "Make the line pass through every point." An "I can't do it" button.

**Learner turns** the dials. The line passes through two points at most. After three tries, or on "I can't do it," the border row reappears.

**Why?** "A straight-line rule adds the same amount every step. These figures add 3, then 5, then 7. No single a works, so no line passes through every point."

## Part 6: From squares to speed

### Screen 13. A square that grows smoothly

**Purpose:** a quantity that changes continuously, linked to the squares.

**Shows:** "Figures come in whole numbers. Now imagine the square growing smoothly." A ball rolling down a ramp: after t seconds it has rolled d = t² metres. The table matches the square tiles:

| t (s) | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| d (m) | 0 | 1 | 4 | 9 | 16 |

**Learner types** the ball's average speed from 3 to 4 seconds.
- **Correct (7):** beside it, the 7-tile border from 3 × 3 to 4 × 4 lights up. "7 metres in 1 second, the same 7 as the border."
- **16:** "That's how far it has rolled after 4 seconds. Speed is distance travelled ÷ time taken."
- **4:** "That's the average from 0 to 4 seconds. We want 3 to 4."

**Why?** "Average speed = distance travelled ÷ time taken. From 3 to 4 seconds the ball goes from 9 m to 16 m, 7 m in 1 second. It's the same 7 as the border, because d = t² is the square pattern with time allowed to be any number."

### Screen 14. Shrink the border

**Purpose:** the speed at an instant, seen in the picture.

**Shows:** a 3-by-3 square and a slider for a small extra length h. The border from 3 to (3 + h) appears as two thin strips, each 3 by h, and a tiny corner square, h by h. A table fills as h shrinks.

| h (s) | 1 | 0.5 | 0.1 | 0.01 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Average speed (m/s) | 7 | ? | 6.1 | 6.01 |

**Step A. Learner types** the average speed for h = 0.5 (distance at 3.5 s: 12.25 m).
- **Correct (6.5):** "3.25 m in 0.5 s."
- **3.25:** "That's the distance travelled. It took 0.5 seconds. Divide."

**Step B. Learner slides** h toward 0. The corner square shrinks almost to nothing. Learner types the speed at exactly 3 seconds.
- **Correct (6):** "6 m/s, the ball's speed at the instant t = 3."
- **6.01:** "That's the average over a tiny gap. What number are these closing in on?"
- **0:** "The border shrinks, but border ÷ h doesn't. Look at 6.5, 6.1, 6.01."

**Why?** "The border is two strips, each 3 × h, plus a corner, h × h, so its area is 6h + h². Divide by the time h: the average speed is 6 + h. As h shrinks, the corner vanishes and the average closes in on 6. That number is the speed at a single instant, the first idea of calculus."

## Part 7: Exit ticket

### Screen 15. The garden (independent evidence)

**Purpose:** transfer from pictures to a real situation, alone.

**Shows:** "A garden starts with 24 plants before any new beds are built. Each new bed adds exactly 6 plants. None are removed. Let n be the number of new beds and P the total number of plants."

**Rules:** typed answers only. No sliders, no hints and no **Why?** until the learner submits.

| Item | Correct | Response after submission if wrong |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Write the rule for P. | P = 24 + 6n | "The 24 plants are there before any beds: that's the constant. Each bed adds 6: that's the coefficient." |
| 2. P when n = 0 | 24 | "With no new beds, only the starting plants are there." |
| 3. P when n = 7 | 66 | "24 + 6 × 7 = 24 + 42." |
| 4. A student writes P = 24 + 6(n − 1). What does it give at n = 0? | 18 | "24 + 6(0 − 1) = 18, not 24: it's shifted by one bed, like Arun's rule." |
| 5. Write a correct rule that uses (n − 1). | P = 30 + 6(n − 1) | "Counting from the first bed, start from its total: 30." |
| 6. Which pattern can be written as T = an + b? (6, 10, 14, 18 / 1, 4, 9, 16 / 2, 4, 8, 16) | 6, 10, 14, 18 | "Only that one adds the same amount each step." |
| 7. Which explanation justifies the rule for every n? | "There are always the same 24 starting plants, and each of the n beds adds exactly 6, so the total is 24 + 6n." | For "It works for n = 0 and n = 7": "That checks two cases. It doesn't explain why it works for every n." |

### Screen 16. Self-check

Ticked by what the learner actually did: found the fixed part and the growing part; found the constant at figure 0; told a first total from a constant; solved for a figure number; tested a rule on more than one figure, including Figure 0; showed two rules are equivalent; recognized a pattern with no common difference; found a speed at a single instant.

## Notes for the build

- **One self-contained HTML file,** like Hollow House: all screens in one data block, one engine.
- **Each screen's data:** prompt, figure, input type, correct answer or answer rule, predicted wrong answers and their responses, the general hint, the second-miss demonstration, and the Why? text.
- **Code draws every figure and computes every number,** including tile and stick counts, the border sizes and the shrinking-border speeds.
- **Computers and phones:** tiles, sticks and sliders large enough for a thumb.

## Defaults you may want to change

- **Second miss:** the screen demonstrates; the learner still types the answer.
- **Parts 5 and 6:** they carry the lesson from linear patterns to the first idea of calculus. Without them the lesson stays within linear rules.
- **Title:** "Seeing the Rule" is a working title.

## Marc's answers, by screen

All five questions are answered and built in.

1. **Finding the fixed part (Screen 2).** Answered: the constant is one colour and each group of the coefficient is a second colour, so every later figure shows one extra group in that colour. Built into Screen 2.
2. **Testing a rule (Screen 6).** Answered: one figure isn't enough, so always try another one, such as n = 0 or n = 10; a rule that works for three figures is correct. Built into Screen 6.
3. **Counting 4 sticks per square (Screen 7).** Answered: test the claim on Figure 2 (8 predicted, 7 counted), then Figure 3, until students see each new square adds 3 and Figure 1 is 1 + 3. Built into Screen 7.
4. **Showing two rules are the same (Screen 8).** Answered: substitute 2 or 3 values into each rule, ask whether they're the same, remind students of the distributive property, then simplify; predicted mistakes are tested by substitution. Built into Screen 8.
5. **Arun's shifted rule (Screen 10).** Answered: by this point students figure it out themselves. In the interactive version they touch and build each rule in colour and see that Arun's builds Figure 2. In class, they explain it (MYP communication); here, an unscored explanation box with a model explanation to compare. Built into Screen 10.
